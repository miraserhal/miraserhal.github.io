PublicationsPublished Work

Publication

Law versus Custom: Can Personal Status Reforms Change Marital Practices?

joint with Maëlys de la Rupelle
Revue d'économie du développement, 2026

PDF version

Laws governing marriage and divorce have multifaceted effects on women. This article examines whether personal status law reforms can change deeply rooted marital customs, exploiting India's 2019 ban on instant triple talaq. This practice let a Muslim man unilaterally and irrevocably end a marriage by pronouncing "talaq" (divorce) three times. Using three waves of National Family Health Survey data with a difference-in-differences framework, we find that the probability of divorce among Muslim women fell slightly after the reform, but the effect vanishes among ever-married women. This is likely reflecting a shift toward other divorce forms such as khula (women-initiated divorce), sometimes prompted by husbands seeking to lower their financial obligations. A near-significant rise in spousal abandonment also emerges following the law. These findings show that prohibiting a specific practice may curb certain harms while generating unintended consequences, underscoring how legal reform often struggles to reshape marital practices embedded in social hierarchies.

Working Paper

The Effect of Banning Instant Triple Divorce on Domestic Violence: Evidence from the Triple Talaq Law in India

joint with Maëlys de la Rupelle (Thema - CY Cergy Paris University)

We investigate whether divorce laws influence domestic violence and women’s bargaining power in the context of a developing country. Exploiting the implementation of the instant triple Talaq law in India, we study the effect of banning the instant triple divorce, practiced by Muslim men, on various forms of domestic violence. We employ a triple difference-in-differences design, comparing ever-married Muslim women to ever-married non-Muslim women, before and after the reform, taking into account a rich structure of fixed effects. We find that the introduction of the law significantly increased physical domestic violence and decision-making authority for Muslim women relative to non-Muslim women following the reform. This aligns with the ‘male backlash’ theory, suggesting that an improvement in women’s situation may not necessarily lead to intended consequences. We also find that the impact of the law depends on the salience of Muslim’s minority status, the rise of the political representation of the Hindu nationalist parties (BJP), and the media exposure at the local level. Additionally, the law’s impact is more significant for younger couples compared to older ones.

Working Paper

Childcare Time in France: The Impact of Parents' Work Time

joint with Olivier Donni (Thema - CY Cergy Paris University)

This paper examines how parents' own and partner's work time affect detailed categories of childcare time using the 2009–2010 French Time Use Survey, in which both parents are observed across one weekday and one weekend diary day. We develop what is, to our knowledge, the first structural framework to model the allocation of childcare time over the course of the week, distinguishing between routine and non-routine activities and estimating both between- and within-type specifications with work time instrumented by hourly wages, socio-professional work-time averages, and departmental unemployment rates. Our findings reveal a consistent trade-off between work time and childcare, with the largest declines concentrated in routine care. Non-routine activities are comparatively preserved, suggesting that parents protect the interactions they view as higher-value while more easily outsourcing or substituting routine tasks. Consistent with this, children’s time in paid childcare services rises with parental work time. Intrahousehold responses differ sharply by gender: When mothers work more, fathers increase childcare—especially non-routine activities—whereas when fathers work more, mothers do not increase their childcare time, with adjustment occurring through paid care. This asymmetry underscores entrenched gender roles and the central role of mothers' labor supply in shaping paternal involvement and demand for formal childcare.

Work in Progress

The Effect of Floods on Gender-Based Violence in India

In this paper, I estimate the effect of flood exposure on crimes against women in India. Combining satellite-derived flood data with the court cases filed in India’s lower judiciary between 2010 and 2017, I exploit plausibly exogenous variation in the timing and intensity of flood events across 576 Indian districts. Using the heterogeneity-robust difference-in-differences estimator of de Chaisemartin and D’Haultfœuille (2024), I find that flood exposure significantly increases filed cases of sexual harassment, domestic violence, and total crimes against women. I additionally show that the share of domestic violence in total court cases rises after floods, indicating a disproportionate gender-specific effect beyond any general increase in criminal activity. The findings highlight the gendered consequences of climate-related disasters and underscore the need for gender-sensitive disaster response policies.
